## Safety, Regulatory, and Operational Considerations

All strategies could contend with safety and compliance due to the inherent risks of HBOT. Regardless of budget, a 2-ATA chamber is a Pressure Vessel for Human Occupancy (PVHO) , and best practice is to follow the ASME PVHO-1 standard for design, fabrication, and testing .   In   the U.S., FDA requires PVHO compliance and NFPA 99 (health care facility   fire   code)   for   HBOT   chambers,   and similar regulations apply in Australia .   The Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) would classify a hyperbaric chamber as a medical device - meaning that to legally use it for treatment, it should ideally be an approved model on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (or used under a special exemption/supervision if it's experimental). Using a second-hand or custom-built unit in a private setting for compassionate use may be possible with a doctor's oversight, but any commercial or clinic use demands compliance (for   instance,   staff   operating   the   chamber   should   have   a   Diploma   in Hyperbaric Operations or similar ). 18 5 19

Fire risk and oxygen handling are paramount. Pressurised oxygen dramatically enhances combustion; even at 2 ATA with air ( ≈ 42% equivalent O₂ partial pressure), materials can ignite faster. Thus, all electrical   components   inside   any   chamber   could   be   rated   for   hyperbaric   O₂   service   or   kept   to   a minimum. Many chambers mitigate fire risk by pressurising with air and delivering pure O₂ via mask/hood , so the chamber interior isn't at high O₂ concentration. This approach should be adopted in our deployment: integrate oxygen breathing systems (like a hood or nasal cannula fed by an O₂ concentrator) rather than filling the whole chamber with pure oxygen. Additionally, pressure relief valves and emergency exhaust controls could be functional - if a patient shows oxygen toxicity or if a fire is detected, the operator should be able to safely and quickly depressurise . (Depressurisation in emergency could be balanced against decompression sickness risk - generally, a rapid drop from 2 ATA to 1 ATA is tolerated in an extreme emergency to save a life, but it's a last resort.)

Another factor is monitoring and failsafes . The Aura Geode system's sensors can augment safety: e.g. continuous CO₂ level monitoring inside to ensure the scrubber is effective (CO₂ buildup can cause toxicity in a closed chamber ), or using the internal camera and AI to watch for signs of patient distress (the AI could alert the operator if the patient looks unconscious or is signalling for help). Power backup is crucial - a chamber should have an uninterruptible power supply for its control systems or at least a manual valve to vent if power loss occurs. The Aura integration should not interfere with primary chamber controls; indeed, the design calls for the wireless modules to be sandboxed from HBOT safety systems , meaning the chamber could operate safely even if the AI or sensors fail. 20 21

In   terms   of operational protocols ,   any   quickly   deployed   solution   could   establish   some   standard procedures: e.g.   daily   pre-use   inspection   (check   seals,   valves,   O₂   supply   levels),   a pressurisation schedule (usually taking 5-10 minutes to go to 2 ATA to allow ears to equalise, etc.), and similar decompression schedule. Given the 60-day daily use, maintenance like cleaning the chamber (especially if patients are immunocompromised, to avoid infection risk) and periodic checks of compressors and oxygen concentrators will be needed.
